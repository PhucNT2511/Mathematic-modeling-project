% ==================== 1. INTRODUCTION ====================
\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Debt collection requires financial institutions to allocate limited
staff time among customers located in different geographical areas.
When in-person visits are necessary, collection officers must decide
which customers to visit, in what order, and on which days. These
decisions influence both travel requirements and the amount of money
that may be recovered. Visiting every customer is often impractical
because travel and service times must fit within daily working-hour
limits. Furthermore, a visit does not guarantee payment, and an
unsuccessful visit may require a subsequent attempt.

This project considers a multi-officer, multi-day debt collection
routing problem in Hanoi. A team of collection officers operates
from a common branch over a finite planning horizon. Each customer
is associated with a target collection amount, an assumed payment
probability, a service duration, and a geographical location.
Each officer's daily route starts and ends at the branch, and total
travel and service time must remain within the available working
hours. Customers may be revisited subject to a maximum visit
allowance, but a successfully collected amount must not be counted
more than once.

The motivation is to allocate collection effort effectively under
limited operational capacity and uncertain payment outcomes.
Prioritizing nearby customers alone may overlook larger expected
recoveries, whereas prioritizing large debts alone may lead to
excessive travel or repeated unsuccessful visits. The problem
therefore combines customer selection, visit scheduling, and route
construction. The primary objective is to maximize expected
recovered money. A secondary objective is to minimize total travel
distance while preserving the primary objective value within a
specified numerical tolerance.

The planning problem is formulated as a mixed-integer linear
programming model and implemented in Julia using JuMP and the
HiGHS solver. Heuristic approaches, including a genetic algorithm,
tabu search, and adaptive large neighborhood search, are also
investigated. The experimental study is designed to compare
solution quality, computational time, and optimality gaps when
valid solver bounds are available.

Two planning policies are considered. A static policy constructs
a plan for the entire horizon before execution. A rolling-horizon
policy updates customer states after each day and reoptimizes the
remaining plan. Customers who have paid are removed from future
visits, while unsuccessful visits reduce their remaining visit
allowances. Simulated payment outcomes provide a common basis
for comparing these policies.

The study uses a synthetic dataset informed by public demographic
and credit-related data, together with geographical and road-network
information. Customer accounts and payment outcomes are simulated
and do not represent an actual bank's collection records.
Payment probabilities are modelling assumptions rather than
estimates validated against historical collection outcomes.
The project therefore evaluates the behaviour and trade-offs of
the proposed methods under controlled scenarios, rather than
claiming verified improvements in real banking operations.

% ==================== 2. MATHEMATICAL MODEL ====================
\section{Mathematical Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Problem Assumptions}
The final implementation relies on the following assumptions.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Full-or-zero payment.} A successful visit to customer $i$
  collects the full target amount $w_i$; an unsuccessful visit collects
  nothing. Partial payments are not modelled.
\item \textbf{Conditional payment probability.} $p_{it}$ is the probability
  that customer $i$ pays when visited on day $t$, given that the customer has
  not paid before. Payment outcomes of different visits are conditionally
  independent, and $p_{it}$ decreases with $t$.
\item \textbf{Maximum visits.} Customer $i$ can be visited at most $m_i$
  times over the horizon. All experiments use $m_i=2$.
\item \textbf{At most one visit per customer per day}, made by exactly one
  officer. Different visits to the same customer may be made by different
  officers.
\item \textbf{Officer eligibility.} Every officer may visit every customer;
  the officers are identical except for their (equal) daily time budgets.
\item \textbf{Daily routes.} Every active officer leaves the branch once per
  day and returns to it before the working-time budget is exhausted. The
  budget covers travel time and service time.
\item \textbf{After a successful payment} the debt is settled: the amount is
  counted once, and the customer receives no further visits. In the static
  plan this is handled during execution (Section~\ref{sec:rolling}); in the
  planning model it is captured by the expected value of a visit schedule.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Input Data and Notation}
Table~\ref{tab:notation} lists the notation. Money is expressed in
million VND, time in hours and distance in kilometers. Travel times and
distances come from shortest (fastest) paths on a directed motorbike road
network, so in general $d_{ij}\ne d_{ji}$ and $\tau_{ij}\ne\tau_{ji}$.

\subsection{Decision Variables}
All variables are indexed by $i,j\in V$, $k\in K$, $t\in T$ and
$S\in\mathcal S_i$.
\begin{itemize}
\item $x_{ijkt}\in\{0,1\}$, $(i,j)\in A$: equals 1 if officer $k$ travels
  directly from node $i$ to node $j$ on day $t$ (arc variables).
\item $y_{ikt}\in\{0,1\}$, $i\in N$: equals 1 if officer $k$ visits customer
  $i$ on day $t$ (visit assignment).
\item $z_{kt}\in\{0,1\}$: equals 1 if officer $k$ performs a route on day
  $t$ (route activation).
\item $\lambda_{iS}\in\{0,1\}$, $i\in N$: equals 1 if customer $i$ is
  visited exactly on the set of days $S$ (schedule selection).
\item $u_{ikt}\ge 0$, $i\in N$: position of customer $i$ in the route of
  officer $k$ on day $t$; used only for subtour elimination
  (Miller--Tucker--Zemlin variables~\cite{mtz1960}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Objective Function}
With full-or-zero payment and conditionally independent visits, the
probability that customer $i$ pays at least once under schedule $S$ is
$1-\prod_{t\in S}(1-p_{it})$, so its expected collection is
\begin{equation}
v_{iS}=w_i\Bigl[1-\prod_{t\in S}\bigl(1-p_{it}\bigr)\Bigr].
\label{eq:schedule-value}
\end{equation}
This value is not additive over the visits: a second visit only adds value
if the first one fails. Expressing it directly in terms of $y_{ikt}$ would
require products of binary variables. Instead, $v_{iS}$ is computed before
solving for every schedule in $\mathcal S_i$, and the model selects one
schedule per customer. The objective is therefore linear:
\begin{equation}
\max\; Z=\sum_{i\in N}\sum_{S\in\mathcal S_i} v_{iS}\,\lambda_{iS}.
\label{eq:objective}
\end{equation}
With $H=5$ days and $m_i=2$, each customer has
$|\mathcal S_i|=\binom51+\binom52=15$ schedules.

\textbf{Secondary objective.} Let $Z^\star$ be the value obtained in the first
solve. A second solve keeps the expected collection within a relative
tolerance $\varepsilon=10^{-6}$ and minimizes the total distance:
\begin{align}
&\sum_{i\in N}\sum_{S\in\mathcal S_i} v_{iS}\lambda_{iS}
  \;\ge\; Z^\star-\varepsilon\max(1,|Z^\star|), \label{eq:lexi}\\
&\min\;\sum_{t\in T}\sum_{k\in K}\sum_{(i,j)\in A} d_{ij}\,x_{ijkt}.
  \label{eq:distance}
\end{align}
The second solve starts from the first solution and has its own time limit.
It was \emph{not} enabled in the algorithm-comparison experiments
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}), where only~\eqref{eq:objective} is
optimized. In the heuristics, distance is always used as a lexicographic
tie-breaker between solutions with equal expected value.

\subsection{Constraints}
\begin{align}
&\sum_{S\in\mathcal S_i}\lambda_{iS}\le 1
  && \forall i\in N, \label{eq:c-schedule}\\
&\sum_{k\in K} y_{ikt}=\sum_{S\in\mathcal S_i} a_{tS}\lambda_{iS}
  && \forall i\in N,\ t\in T, \label{eq:c-link}\\
&\sum_{j\in N} x_{0jkt}=z_{kt},\qquad \sum_{i\in N} x_{i0kt}=z_{kt}
  && \forall k\in K,\ t\in T, \label{eq:c-depot}\\
&\sum_{j\in V\setminus\{i\}} x_{ijkt}=y_{ikt},\qquad
 \sum_{j\in V\setminus\{i\}} x_{jikt}=y_{ikt}
  && \forall i\in N,\ k\in K,\ t\in T, \label{eq:c-flow}\\
&\sum_{(i,j)\in A}\tau_{ij}x_{ijkt}+\sum_{i\in N}s_i y_{ikt}\le B_{kt}z_{kt}
  && \forall k\in K,\ t\in T, \label{eq:c-time}\\
&u_{ikt}-u_{jkt}+n\,x_{ijkt}\le n-1+n(1-y_{jkt})
  && \forall i,j\in N,\ i\ne j,\ k\in K,\ t\in T, \label{eq:c-mtz}\\
&y_{ikt}\le u_{ikt}\le n\,y_{ikt}
  && \forall i\in N,\ k\in K,\ t\in T, \label{eq:c-order}\\
&x_{ijkt},\,y_{ikt},\,z_{kt},\,\lambda_{iS}\in\{0,1\},\quad u_{ikt}\ge 0. \label{eq:c-domain}
\end{align}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Schedule selection}~\eqref{eq:c-schedule}: each customer gets at
  most one schedule. The inequality allows a customer to be skipped. The
  visit limit $m_i$ is enforced by the definition of $\mathcal S_i$.
\item \textbf{Schedule--visit consistency}~\eqref{eq:c-link}: if the chosen
  schedule contains day $t$, exactly one officer visits customer $i$ on that
  day; otherwise nobody does. This also rules out two visits to the same
  customer on the same day.
\item \textbf{Branch departure and return}~\eqref{eq:c-depot}: an active
  officer leaves and re-enters the branch exactly once per day; an inactive
  officer uses no arc.
\item \textbf{Flow conservation}~\eqref{eq:c-flow}: a visited customer has
  exactly one incoming and one outgoing arc on the route of the officer who
  visits them, and none on other routes.
\item \textbf{Working-time limit}~\eqref{eq:c-time}: travel plus service time
  of officer $k$ on day $t$ does not exceed $B_{kt}$; if $z_{kt}=0$ the officer
  does no work that day.
\item \textbf{Subtour elimination}~\eqref{eq:c-mtz}--\eqref{eq:c-order}: if
  $x_{ijkt}=1$ and $j$ is visited, then $u_{jkt}\ge u_{ikt}+1$, so positions
  strictly increase along a route and no cycle disconnected from the branch
  can exist. The term $n(1-y_{jkt})$ deactivates the constraint when $j$ is
  not on the route, and~\eqref{eq:c-order} forces $u_{ikt}=0$ for unvisited
  customers.
\item \textbf{Domains}~\eqref{eq:c-domain}.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Symmetry breaking.} Officers with the same budget are interchangeable:
relabelling two officers gives a different solution with the same value, and
the branch-and-bound tree explores such duplicates. For consecutive officers
with $B_{kt}=B_{k+1,t}$ the code adds
\begin{equation}
z_{kt}\ge z_{k+1,t}\qquad k=1,\dots,|K|-1,\ t\in T,
\label{eq:c-symmetry}
\end{equation}
which removes duplicates without changing the optimal value. It was enabled
in all algorithm-comparison experiments.

\subsection{Rolling-Horizon Operation}
\label{sec:rolling}
The model above produces a \emph{static} plan for the whole horizon. During
execution, payment outcomes are revealed day by day. The state known at the
start of day $t$ is $(P_t,a_t)$, where $P_t$ is the set of customers who have
already paid and $a_{it}$ is the number of visits made to customer $i$
before day $t$. The eligible customers are
$A_t=\{i\in N: i\notin P_t,\ a_{it}<m_i\}$.

The rolling-horizon policy repeats the following steps for $t=1,\dots,H$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Build the remaining problem: customers $A_t$, days $t,\dots,H$,
  probabilities $p_{i,t..H}$, budgets $B_{k,t..H}$, remaining visit
  allowances $m_i-a_{it}$, and the travel sub-matrix of the branch and the
  customers in $A_t$. The probabilities are not re-estimated: because
  $p_{it}$ is already conditional on no earlier payment, it remains valid for
  every customer still in $A_t$.
\item Solve the same model~\eqref{eq:objective}--\eqref{eq:c-domain} over the
  remaining horizon.
\item Execute only the routes of day $t$; the plan for later days is
  discarded.
\item Observe the simulated payments of the visited customers, add the payers
  to $P_{t+1}$ and increase $a_{i,t+1}$ for every visited customer.
\end{enumerate}
Two further policies serve as references. The \emph{static} policy solves
once at the start and executes the whole plan; a customer who has already
paid is skipped, and the officer goes straight to the next unpaid customer
on the route without inserting new customers. The \emph{myopic} policy works
like the rolling horizon but optimizes only day $t$ each morning
($H=1$ in the remaining problem), so constraint~\eqref{eq:c-link} no longer
links it to later days.

All three policies produce feasible routes. The rolling-horizon procedure is
\emph{not} a fully adaptive stochastic model: each re-solve maximizes the
expected collection as if the remaining plan were executed without further
re-planning. It does not anticipate that future decisions will depend on
future outcomes, and it is not an optimal policy of the underlying
multi-stage stochastic problem. Solver bounds certify only the remaining
problem at each re-solve, not the policy.

% ==================== 3. SOLUTION METHODS ====================
\section{Solution Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{MILP Solution with HiGHS}
The model is built with JuMP~\cite{jump2023} and solved by the
branch-and-cut algorithm of HiGHS~\cite{highs2018}, with all of
$x,y,z,\lambda$ declared binary. HiGHS performs presolve, solves the LP
relaxation at the root node, adds cutting planes, runs its primal
heuristics and branches on fractional variables, while maintaining an
incumbent and an upper bound on~\eqref{eq:objective}. The solver stops when
the relative gap falls below $10^{-4}$ (\texttt{mip\_rel\_gap}) or when the
time limit is reached: 30\,s for the one-day problem and 150\,s for the
five-day problem (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}). The HiGHS random seed is set
to the seed of each run. The reported status is \texttt{OPTIMAL} or
\texttt{TIME\_LIMIT}, and the bound and branch-and-bound node count are read
from the solver after the main solve.

\textbf{Warm start.} In the warm-started variant, an ALNS solution obtained
with the same seed is passed to HiGHS as a MIP start (values of $x,y,z,u$
and of the matching $\lambda_{iS}$). The ALNS time is not included in the
solver time. If HiGHS stops without a solution at least as good as the
start, the code returns the ALNS solution and labels the run
\texttt{TIME\_LIMIT\_HEURISTIC\_FALLBACK}. Such a solution is reported as an
ALNS solution, not as one found by HiGHS; this happened in 6 of the 70
warm-started five-day runs (1 on INS\_S with 50 customers, 2 on REP1\_S and
3 on REP2\_S). The solver bound remains valid in all cases.

\textbf{Shared heuristic framework.} The three metaheuristics below use the
same representation: an assignment matrix
$G\in\{0,1,\dots,|K|\}^{n\times H}$, where $g_{it}=0$ means that customer $i$
is not visited on day $t$ and $g_{it}=k$ means that officer $k$ visits them.
A cell holds one value, so a customer cannot be visited by two officers on
the same day. For every officer--day pair, the route through the assigned
customers is built by nearest-neighbor from the branch and improved by
2-opt, where every reversal is evaluated on the full (asymmetric) distance.
A shared \emph{repair} procedure restores feasibility: if a customer has more
than $m_i$ visits, only the $m_i$ days with the highest $p_{it}$ are kept; if
a route exceeds $B_{kt}$, the visit with the smallest marginal loss
$v_{iS_i}-v_{i,S_i\setminus\{t\}}$ is removed and the route rebuilt, until
the route fits. Solutions are compared lexicographically: higher expected
collection first, then shorter total distance. Because the order within a
route is built by a rule rather than optimized, the heuristics may miss
routes that the MILP can find, and they provide no optimality certificate.

\subsection{Genetic Algorithm}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Encoding:} one individual is one matrix $G$.
\item \textbf{Initialization:} each cell is selected for a visit with
  probability 0.35 and assigned to a random officer; the individual is then
  repaired.
\item \textbf{Selection:} tournament selection of size 3, based on the
  lexicographic comparison above.
\item \textbf{Crossover:} uniform crossover; each cell of the child is copied
  from one of the two parents with equal probability.
\item \textbf{Mutation:} each cell is replaced, with probability 0.03, by 0 or
  a random officer.
\item \textbf{Repair and elitism:} every child is repaired; the 4 best
  individuals are copied unchanged into the next generation.
\item \textbf{Parameters and stopping:} population 80, at most 300
  generations, stopping early after 50 generations without improvement of
  the best solution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Tabu Search}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Initial solution:} candidate visits $(i,t)$ are ranked by
  $w_ip_{it}/(s_i+\tau_{0i}+\tau_{i0})$ and inserted greedily while the
  solution stays feasible.
\item \textbf{Neighborhood:} a move picks a random cell $(i,t)$ and changes
  its value to ``no visit'' or to another officer, i.e.\ it adds, removes or
  reassigns one visit. Each iteration samples 40 such moves, repairs and
  evaluates them, and moves to the best admissible neighbor even if it is
  worse than the current solution. A neighbor whose repair undoes the
  proposed change is discarded.
\item \textbf{Tabu memory:} returning a cell to its previous value is
  forbidden for 15 iterations.
\item \textbf{Aspiration:} a tabu move is allowed if it yields a new best
  solution.
\item \textbf{Stopping:} at most 300 iterations, or 50 consecutive iterations
  without improving the best solution.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Adaptive Large Neighborhood Search}
ALNS~\cite{ropke2006} removes a large part of the plan and rebuilds it.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Destroy operators} (remove about 20\% of the current visits):
  random removal; worst-value removal (lowest marginal expected value);
  related removal (a random customer and the customers closest to them);
  route removal (part of one officer--day route).
\item \textbf{Repair operators:} greedy insertion, which inserts the feasible
  triple $(i,t,k)$ with the largest gain in expected value, breaking ties by
  the smallest increase in distance; and regret-2 insertion, which favors
  customers who lose the most if their best insertion is not used. Customers
  not in the plan may be inserted. Insertion continues until no feasible
  insertion remains.
\item \textbf{Operator selection and weights:} roulette-wheel selection by
  operator weight. Operators score 10 for a new global best, 5 for improving
  the current solution and 1 for an accepted solution. Every 25 iterations
  the weights are updated as $w\leftarrow 0.8\,w+0.2\,\bar\pi$, where
  $\bar\pi$ is the mean score in the segment (floored at 0.1).
\item \textbf{Acceptance:} simulated annealing on the score
  $Z/\sum_i w_i - 10^{-4}\times\text{km}$, with initial temperature 0.05 and
  cooling factor 0.995 per iteration; the best solution is still kept by the
  lexicographic comparison.
\item \textbf{Stopping:} at most 300 iterations, or 50 consecutive iterations
  without improving the best solution.
\end{itemize}

\section{Numerical Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Dataset and Test Instances}
The dataset~\cite{projectrepo} combines three kinds of inputs.

\textbf{Observed public data.} The road network, the 2025 ward boundaries and
populations, building density and the branch location (a bank point of
interest in central Hanoi) come from OpenStreetMap~\cite{osm}. After keeping
the largest strongly connected motorbike network, the graph has 57{,}242
nodes and 130{,}220 directed edges, 6.6\% of them one-way. Demographic
profiles (gender, age, marital status, education, occupation) are taken
unchanged from NVIDIA Nemotron-Personas-Vietnam~\cite{nemotron}.

\textbf{Synthetic inputs informed by public data.} Customer locations are
sampled in 27 wards with quotas proportional to ward population, at road
nodes weighted by nearby building density. The shape of the credit-limit and
card-utilization distributions is taken from the UCI Default of Credit Card
Clients dataset~\cite{uci2009} (customers at least two months late), and the
shares of debt groups 2--5 follow published Vietnamese bank reports. Loan
amounts, interest rates and terms of other products are drawn from assumed
log-normal distributions. The target amount $w_i$ is the overdue amount.

\textbf{Modelling assumptions.} The payment probabilities
$p_{it}=h\,p_0(g)\,(1-0.3f_i)\,(1-\delta(g))^{t-1}$, capped at 0.95, depend
only on the debt group $g$, the position $f_i$ of the customer's days past
due within the group, and the day $t$; $h=0.5/1/1.5$ defines the
\emph{low}, \emph{base} and \emph{high} scenarios (THAP, CO\_SO, CAO).
Road speeds by road type, service times, the 480-minute working day and the
planning period (five working days) are also assumed. The probabilities are
not calibrated against real collection outcomes.

The model-layer graph is complete and directed: $|V|=n+1$ nodes and
$|A|=n(n+1)$ arcs, where each arc carries the time and distance of the
fastest road path, computed with Dijkstra's algorithm. The matrix is
asymmetric for 95.8\% of the node pairs. Figure~\ref{fig:graph} illustrates
the two graph layers.

customers of INS\_XS ($n=10,20,30$) or INS\_S ($n=50,80$). REP1\_S--REP4\_S
(one-day problem) and REP1\_S--REP2\_S (five-day problem) are added at $n=80$

\subsection{Computational Environment}

\subsection{Experimental Protocol}
\textbf{Algorithm comparison (repeated optimization runs).} The study has two
phases, mirroring the single-period and multi-period steps of the course's
reference problem: \emph{Phase~1} solves a one-day problem ($H=1$, so each
customer is visited at most once and no day linking occurs) and
\emph{Phase~2} the five-day problem ($H=5$). Each configuration is solved in
10 independent runs $r=1,\dots,10$ with seed $2026+r$, used by every method
of that run, and all reported values are averages over the 10 runs. The
HiGHS time limit is proportional to the number of days, as in the reference
problem: 30\,s in Phase~1 and $150\,\text{s}=5\times30$\,s in Phase~2, the
same for every instance of a phase. The metaheuristics are not given a time
limit but the same iteration budget: at most 300 iterations or generations,
stopping after 50 consecutive iterations without improvement (parameters in
Section~\ref{sec:methods}). In Phase~2, HiGHS is run twice per run: cold
(no start) and warm-started from the ALNS solution of the same seed.

\textbf{Timing.} For HiGHS, the reported time is the wall-clock time of the
\texttt{optimize!} call; reading the CSV files, building the instance and
building the JuMP model are excluded, and so is the ALNS run that provides
the warm start. For the metaheuristics, the time is the wall-clock time of
the solver call (construction, search and repair). Julia compiles each function on
its first call, and this compilation time is included in the first run of
the first instance (for example, Tabu took 5.8\,s in run 1 on the
10-customer Phase~2 instance against a mean of 0.72\,s). Total elapsed time
per run, including model construction, was not recorded separately.

\textbf{Policy comparison (Monte Carlo payment scenarios).} These scenarios
are distinct from the repeated optimization runs: the routing method is fixed
(ALNS, seed 2026, 300 iterations) and the uncertainty comes from the payment
outcomes. The instance is INS\_S (80 customers, 2 officers, 5 days,
$m_i=2$), evaluated under the three probability scenarios with 20 payment
scenarios $s=1,\dots,20$ each. The dataset stores a uniform number
$\xi^{(s)}_{it}\in[0,1]$ for every customer and day; a visit to customer $i$
on day $t$ in scenario $s$ succeeds if $\xi^{(s)}_{it}<p_{it}$, and the
outcome is only drawn when the visit is actually made. The same numbers are
used by all policies (common random numbers), so the paired differences are
due to the policies alone. The algorithms never see $\xi^{(s)}$ when
planning; it is used only to generate the outcome after the routes of a day
are chosen. In the static policy, a planned visit to a customer who has
already paid is skipped: the officer drives straight to the next unpaid
customer on the route, and the freed time is not used. The static plan was
solved once per probability scenario with all 300 ALNS iterations, whereas
each daily re-solve of the rolling-horizon and myopic policies used the
early-stopping rule (50 iterations without improvement).

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Expected collection $Z$} (million VND): the value of
  objective~\eqref{eq:objective} for the returned plan, i.e.\ the
  expectation \emph{before} execution, not an amount that is certain to be
  collected.
\item \textbf{Simulated collected money} (million VND): the sum of $w_i$ over
  the successful visits in one payment scenario, averaged over the scenarios.
  Only this quantity is used to compare policies.
\item \textbf{Distance} (km): total length of the executed routes in the
  policy comparison. Distances were not recorded in the algorithm-comparison
  runs, which optimize only $Z$.
\item \textbf{Time} (s): as defined in the protocol above.
\item \textbf{Optimality gap.} In each run, $UB^\ast$ is the tightest (smallest)
  upper bound returned by the HiGHS calls of that run (cold and, in
  Phase~2, warm), and the gap of every method in that run is
  \begin{equation}
  \mathrm{gap}=\frac{UB^\ast-Z}{Z}\times 100\%.
  \label{eq:gap}
  \end{equation}
  The denominator is the solution value, as in the course's reference
  formula; values below $10^{-9}$ are reported as 0. A gap of 0 means the
  solution is proved optimal. For the heuristics the gap is conservative:
  when $UB^\ast$ is loose, the true distance to the optimum may be smaller. A
  gap is reported only if both a feasible solution and a finite bound exist;
  otherwise it is left empty and never treated as 0. In all reported runs,
  every method returned a feasible solution and HiGHS returned a finite
  bound.
\item \textbf{Branch-and-bound nodes:} the HiGHS node count
  (\texttt{MOI.NodeCount}) of the main solve; 0 or 1 means that the solver
  stopped at the root node. N/A for the heuristics.
\item \textbf{Optimal runs:} the number of runs, out of 10, in which HiGHS
  reported status \texttt{OPTIMAL} (gap $\le 10^{-4}$).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Discussion}
\textbf{Small instances.} Up to 30 customers, HiGHS proves optimality in
10/10 runs in both phases, and the three metaheuristics find the same
optimal value in every run. The one-day problem is solved at the root node,
whereas the five-day problem with 30 customers needs on average 56.5 nodes
and 19.6\,s. With the same number of customers, linking the days through
the schedule variables makes the problem clearly harder.

\textbf{Behavior as size grows.} The model size grows roughly as
$|K|\,H\,n^2$ because of the arc variables, from 2{,}012 variables
($n=30$, one day) to 67{,}610 ($n=80$, five days). From $n=50$ on, HiGHS
reaches its time limit after at most 3.5 nodes on average in both phases:
almost all the
time is spent at the root node, and the incumbent is poor (gaps of 271\% to
16{,}594\% on INS\_S), with a large variation between runs. The bound
itself is informative, since the heuristics lie within 0.1--3.7\% of it.
These gaps therefore mostly reflect the solver's weak incumbent, not a weak
bound.

\textbf{Quality versus runtime.} The metaheuristics reach gaps of 2.2--3.7\%
in Phase~1 and 0.4--2.6\% in Phase~2 at $n=80$, in 2 to 40\,s. In Phase~2,
ALNS gives the smallest gap on every instance with $n\ge50$ (0.08--0.49\%)
and the smallest variation between runs; Tabu is the fastest (5--7\,s) with
a gap of about 1\%; GA is slower than Tabu, and at $n=80$ it has the largest
gap (1.6--2.6\%) and the largest variation. In Phase~1
the three methods are within about 0.5 percentage points of each other, and
none is best on all instances. The conclusions are the same on INS\_S and
on the independent REP sets (Table~\ref{tab:replicates}). Since the
heuristics stop by an iteration rule while HiGHS runs to its time limit,
the comparison is one of quality at the observed runtime, not at equal time.

\textbf{Warm start.} Starting HiGHS from the ALNS solution gives exactly the
ALNS value and gap: within 150\,s HiGHS neither improves the start nor
closes the gap. On the 30-customer instance, the warm start reduces the
solver time from 19.6\,s to 1.9\,s, because the optimal solution is provided
from the start.

\textbf{Benefits and limits of re-optimization.} Re-planning every day does
not change the simulated collection significantly on INS\_S: all confidence
intervals for the collection difference contain 0, and the rolling horizon
is better than static in only 10--12 of the 20 scenarios. Its advantage does
grow with the payment probability ($-0.4\%$, $+0.4\%$, $+0.7\%$), which is
expected: more payments mean more freed capacity and more information. The
clear benefit is travel: the rolling horizon collects about the same amount
with 6--9\% less distance in every scenario group, because it drops planned
repeat visits to customers who have already paid. It is also faster per
scenario, since the remaining problems shrink. Two factors limit the gain.
First, each re-solve optimizes an expectation as if no further re-planning
would happen, so the policy is not optimal for the multi-stage problem.
Second, ALNS is a heuristic, and the quality of consecutive solves varies by
an amount comparable to the re-planning gain; in addition, the static plan
received the full 300 ALNS iterations while each daily re-solve stopped
early.

\textbf{Variability.} The collected money varies far more between payment
scenarios (standard deviation 150--210 million VND) than between policies,
which is why paired differences on common random numbers are needed. The
myopic policy travels least, because it never plans repeat visits for later
days, but its paired difference has a standard deviation 1.5--2.2 times that
of the rolling horizon (22--45 against 14--28 million VND): it sometimes wins
by a large margin and sometimes loses by one. With only 20 scenarios, none
of the three policies can be called superior in collected money.

% ==================== 5. CONCLUSION ====================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This project studied a multi-officer, multi-day debt collection routing
problem on the road network of Hanoi, with the goal of maximizing expected
recovered money under daily working-time limits and a maximum number of
visits per customer. The nonlinear value of repeated visits was linearized by
selecting one precomputed visit schedule per customer, which gives a MILP
with arc, assignment, route-activation, schedule and MTZ variables, plus
symmetry breaking and an optional lexicographic distance objective. The
model was solved with HiGHS and compared with a genetic algorithm, tabu
search and ALNS, and a static plan was compared with rolling-horizon and
myopic re-planning on common simulated payment scenarios.

The measured results suggest the following use of the methods. Up to 30
customers, HiGHS proves optimality within seconds and should be preferred,
since it provides a certificate. From 50 customers on, HiGHS does not leave
the root node within 30--150\,s and its incumbent is unusable, although its
bound remains useful to assess the heuristics. At this size the
metaheuristics are the practical choice: ALNS gives the best plans for the
five-day problem (gap 0.08--0.49\%), and Tabu gives slightly weaker plans
several times faster. Warm-starting HiGHS from ALNS keeps the ALNS quality
but did not improve it within the time limit. In the policy comparison on
80 customers, daily re-planning did not change the simulated collection
significantly, but it reduced travel distance by 6--9\%.

These findings are limited by the data. Customer accounts are synthetic,
the payment probabilities and their decline over time are modelling
assumptions not calibrated on real collection outcomes, travel speeds
ignore congestion, and payments are full or nothing. The size of the
re-planning gain in particular depends on the assumed probabilities. The
experiments also cover at most 80 customers and two officers, and the
policy comparison uses only 20 payment scenarios.

Two directions are realistic next steps. First, running the policy
comparison with more scenarios (the dataset provides 100) and on the larger
instances INS\_M and INS\_L would show whether re-planning yields a
significant gain in collection at larger scale. Second, the heuristic
routes could be improved and the bounds tightened, for example by stronger
subtour-elimination cuts than MTZ or a decomposition by day, so that exact
methods remain usable beyond 30 customers.
